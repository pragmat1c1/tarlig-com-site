Enterprise GenAI platform in a regulated healthcare setting

A publicly shareable summary of platform work I owned internally: evaluation, enterprise hardening, and the production rollout of a GenAI assistant capability.

Context

As the internal lead for GenAI platform delivery in a regulated clinical environment, I owned the evaluation, enterprise hardening, and production rollout of a GenAI assistant platform end to end. This work sat at the intersection of AI capability, existing enterprise IT, regulated data boundaries, stakeholder trust, and real operational adoption. The goal was never a demo, but a platform path that can be operated, governed, and adopted across the organization.

The approach followed a principle I hold generally: buy the engine, build the integration. The model and the platform base came from proven, managed building blocks. The differentiating part was embedding it into a regulated enterprise: identity, data boundaries, security, knowledge integration, and rollout.

How the project started

The platform originated in the context of innovation management, as one of several AI initiatives. I presented my concept to an innovation board of executives and senior management; it was selected as one of several AI use cases for implementation.

The obvious route would have been a classic consulting project: a six-figure budget and roughly 18 months. After a make-or-buy analysis, I delivered it in-house and lean instead: I selected a proven reference architecture on AWS as the base and, on top of it, owned the entire enterprise hardening, deployment, and ongoing development myself, including self-built crawlers for knowledge integration. Start small, ship early, iterate on real user feedback, ownership over a requirements spec. That kept architecture and operations in-house and brought the platform into production at roughly 80 percent below the originally estimated budget.

What I owned

  • Evaluation of the GenAI capability against concrete workflow and governance requirements.
  • Architecture decisions, platform boundaries, integration points, and operational ownership.
  • Enterprise hardening for security, identity, data handling, and observability.
  • The rollout path from controlled introduction to broad organizational use.

Architecture overview

The platform is a serverless three-tier architecture on AWS. It runs without its own servers, scales with load, and keeps operational overhead small. The building blocks below are deliberately at the architecture level, without internal detail.

Frontend and delivery

The frontend is a React SPA, delivered as static files from S3 via CloudFront. No dedicated web server, no patching of a web tier. CloudFront handles path-based routing to the backends.

Authentication and permissions

Sign-in runs through Cognito with federation to the corporate identity provider via OIDC. Employees log in with their existing corporate account, no separate credentials. On login, a pre-token function maps identity-provider groups to role-based permissions in the platform, for example who may create or administer bots. Federation credentials live in Secrets Manager, never in code.

Two API channels: CRUD and streaming

The platform separates two load profiles cleanly. An HTTP API channel serves synchronous CRUD operations (conversations, bot configurations, settings, administration) through a Lambda-based API. A second channel over WebSocket delivers model responses in real time, token by token, through a dedicated streaming function. The interface stays responsive while long generations run.

RAG with source grounding

Answers rely not only on the model's knowledge, but on real internal documents. Self-built crawlers keep the knowledge base current and sync approved sources (intranet, QMS, SOPs, wikis) into S3. A managed knowledge base splits documents into chunks, creates embeddings, and stores the vectors in an OpenSearch Serverless vector database. On a query, semantically relevant passages are retrieved and passed to the model as context. Answers carry citations back to the source documents, so statements stay verifiable. The real work here was less the language model than the data pipeline: identifying distributed, often unstructured knowledge, making it accessible, preparing it, and masking personal data. A rule of thumb from the project: the model is 20 percent of success, the data pipeline 80 percent. Why source grounding is not a detail but an architecture requirement, I have described elsewhere.

Security and data boundaries

A WAF sits in front of the platform at CloudFront: an IP allowlist for authorized networks, rate limiting, and managed rules against the OWASP Top 10. Only approved networks reach the application. Sensitive configuration lives in Secrets Manager. Data boundaries, permissions, and logging are design constraints, not afterthoughts.

Data storage

  • DynamoDB for conversations, messages, and bot configurations (fast key-value access).
  • OpenSearch Serverless for RAG vectors and a searchable bot store.
  • S3 for the frontend and the raw documents of the knowledge base.
  • Secrets Manager for credentials and keys.

Enterprise hardening for a regulated environment

In a regulated environment, compliance is not a feature you add later, but a design constraint from day 1. Concretely that meant: clear data boundaries and access roles, source grounding instead of free assertion, traceability of answers, logging and observability for usage and cost, and human control where output becomes consequential. These requirements shaped the architecture more than any model benchmark.

Adoption and rollout

A platform nobody uses has failed, no matter how cleanly it is built. The path led from a controlled introduction to around 750 active users, with a path toward a group-wide rollout to more than 18,000 employees. A bot marketplace lets business units create and share specialized assistants themselves instead of waiting for a central team: assistants for knowledge navigation, quality management and SOPs, domain guides, IT support, or line-of-business applications, plus a guided bot-creator for self-service. Usage analytics and role-based administration keep operations governable. Enablement, clear use cases, and support were part of the work, not an afterthought.

Secure handover

Production means the system keeps running even when I am no longer in the room. Over six months I handed the platform over in a structured way, with workshops, coaching, and hands-on sessions, to an internal team of two engineers and two business users plus an external service provider for ongoing operations. The goal was secure, self-sufficient platform operation without dependence on a single person.

Result

An AI ambition turned into an operated, governed, and genuinely used system. Delivered in-house, compliant from day 1, and on a stack that stays interchangeable: models can be swapped without rebuilding the platform.

Role

Solution architecture, decision frameworks, stakeholder alignment, and delivery, as the accountable internal lead, with hands-on technical judgment and executive context: enough detail to make the platform real, enough abstraction to keep decisions legible for leadership.
